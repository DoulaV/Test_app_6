# Copy Sharpener

Given a draft or a brief plus an audience, run the pre-writing research (empathy map, their verbatim words, positioning, promise, proof, priority), then produce or rewrite the copy with every line passed through the five sentence tests, score the hook, and close with one call to action for one person.

Learned from: How To Write Words That Make People Buy (Full Course) (https://youtu.be/OP_eMk_RCWg). The timestamped beat sheet is
in `references/source-notes.md`; anything copied from the screen is in
`references/verbatim-assets.md`.

## Where this sits among the other skills

This skill owns the words. The other four own everything around them, and the
overlap is real, so the division is fixed:

| Question | Skill |
|---|---|
| Who am I and what am I known for? | `brand-architect` |
| Who am I for and what should I make? | `algorithm-strategist` |
| Which idea, validated how, with what proof? | `brand-idea-miner` |
| What are the first three lines? | `hook-writer` |
| How is the body structured so nobody leaves? | `story-loop-writer` |
| Are these the right words, in the right order, at the right length? | this skill |

Run it last, on whatever the others produced, and on anything they do not
touch: captions, bios, landing pages, emails, DMs, ad copy, on-screen text.
Two places it reaches back:

- **Hook scoring.** `hook-writer` generates hooks from the three-step formula.
  This skill scores them with benefit times relevance times credibility over
  perceived effort. Generate there, score here, and fix the weak variable.
- **Proof.** `brand-idea-miner` writes the proof plan. This skill runs that
  proof through the deaf and mute test before it reaches the page.

`brand-architect` is the layer above all five. Its association sentence and
brand story are what the 6Ps positioning and promise should be consistent
with, so when the user has both, the brand definition wins over anything
invented here. When they have neither and the copy keeps coming out generic,
the problem is usually identity rather than wording, and it belongs there.

`humanizer` runs after this skill, as the last pass over finished prose. This
skill decides what the words say and how they land; humanizer strips the
residue that says a model wrote them. It handles English and Egyptian Arabic
with separate pattern sets, because the failure modes are opposite: English AI
prose is padded with staging to cut, Egyptian AI prose is Modern Standard
Arabic in costume and needs its grammar and particles restored. On a cinematic
script tell it the register is Dramatic, or it will add chat-register
performance that does not belong in a character's mouth.

## Prerequisites

- The draft or the brief, who it is for, and what you want the reader to do after reading. When the audience is not yet fixed, run algorithm-strategist first; this skill sharpens words, it does not choose the audience.

## Procedure

1. **Do the thinking before the writing: run the 6Ps (people, positioning, promise, proof, priority, process) and refuse to draft until you have more material than you can use.** Weak copy is almost never a writing failure, it is a thinking failure. You cannot answer why they should choose this until you know who they are. The game is won before the pen is used, and the one thing a model cannot do for you is dig up the specific, unstated facts about this offer and this buyer. (see source-notes: t=02:40, t=03:30, t=06:10, t=10:40)
2. **Build the empathy map in four quadrants: what their daily reality is (doing, seeing, hearing, feeling), what frustrates them daily, what they fear ending up as, and what they want to become.** The platinum rule beats the golden rule: treat people the way they want to be treated, which means standing in their shoes rather than putting them in yours. The same product needs five different promises for five different avatars, and only the map tells you which fear you are speaking to. (see source-notes: t=03:45, t=05:02, t=05:25)
3. **Harvest the audience's verbatim language and write it back to them. Pull from recorded sales calls, or from comments, DMs, replies, reviews, and voice notes, and lift their exact phrases into the hooks.** This is the highest-leverage move in the whole method. If you can describe someone's pain better than they can, they conclude you must have the solution. The pain is the pitch. Invented hooks compete with the reader's guard; their own sentences walk straight past it. (see source-notes: t=06:20, t=06:33, t=08:40)
4. **Find the positioning by naming the true thing nobody in the category bothers to say.** Schlitz was last in beer while competing on the word pure, which every brand claimed. Hopkins toured the factory, found the artesian wells and the filters and the mother yeast, and advertised the process. They won without changing anything, because they were the first to state the facts. Every offer has a boring true detail that its owner thinks is not worth mentioning; that detail is usually the position. (see source-notes: t=08:46, t=09:30, t=10:10)
5. **Run every feature up the So What ladder until it becomes a state the reader wants to be in, and write that state rather than the feature.** People buy who they become, not what they get. A silk rug is not a promise; feeling like someone with great taste and a home worth showing off is. Keep asking so what until the answer is about identity or relief, then stop. If you are not clear on the core outcome, the reader will not be either, and a confused reader leaves. (see source-notes: t=10:49, t=11:14, t=11:55)
6. **Attach proof that matches the reader and survives the deaf and mute test: if you could not speak and could only point, what could you point at?** Trust is belief with evidence; belief without evidence is faith, and strangers do not extend faith. You cannot point at trusted by industry leaders, but you can point at the logos. You cannot point at proven results, but you can point at the number. Proof is also relative: a case study only lands when the person in it resembles the person reading it. Client results outrank your own results, which outrank borrowed authority. (see source-notes: t=12:00, t=12:10, t=12:45, t=13:15)
7. **Give one reason to act now: scarcity, a deadline, or the cost of staying put.** People jog toward pleasure and sprint away from pain. Cost of inaction is usually the strongest of the three because it widens the gap between where they are and where they want to be, and that gap is what produces motivation. A drowning person does not casually swim upward. (see source-notes: t=13:54, t=14:05, t=14:25)
8. **Only now write, and put every line through the five tests: clear, concise, concrete, conversational, cadence.** Assume the reader is busy, distracted, and reading below a fifth-grade level, because it is not about how smart the writer looks, it is about how smart the reader feels. Clear: do not add clarity, delete what is unclear, working backwards from the end. Concise: price every word at ten dollars and ask what can be cut without losing quality or understanding. Concrete: make it visualizable, and when the subject is abstract, zoom in until it can be pictured. Conversational: match the reader's own register, read it aloud, and cut anything nobody would say. Cadence: vary sentence length so it reads like music, because bored readers do not buy. (see source-notes: t=19:49, t=20:35, t=21:00, t=22:20, t=23:25, t=24:20, t=25:20)
9. **Score the hook rather than guessing it: benefit times relevance times credibility, divided by perceived effort.** Five times as many people read the headline as the body, so once the headline is written you have spent most of your budget. The formula tells you which variable is weak: no reason to care, too narrow an audience, no reason to believe you, or too much work implied. Raise the weak one instead of rewriting blindly. (see source-notes: t=27:38, t=28:05, t=28:35)
10. **Structure the body as What / Why / How when teaching, or as But / Therefore when telling a story.** Educational copy needs the point, the reason to care, and the way to do it, in that order. Stories told as and then, and then have no tension; alternating but and therefore forces a cause-and-effect arc and keeps a question open. (see source-notes: t=29:18, t=29:25, t=30:10)
11. **Close with a single call to action naming who it is for, what to do, by when, and how. One call to action, one avatar, one offer.** Content that asks for nothing gets nothing. Naming the reader filters out everyone else and makes the right person feel addressed; naming the deadline and the mechanism removes the two most common reasons people intend to act and never do. Two asks split the response and usually get neither. (see source-notes: t=30:50, t=31:00, t=31:25)

## Decision rules

- When the user brings a draft and wants it better: run the five sentence tests line by line and show the before and after for each change, then check whether the promise, proof and call to action exist at all; most drafts fail on concise and concrete first (see source-notes: t=19:49, t=22:20)
- When the copy is for emotional, artistic, or personal content with nothing to sell: keep the 5Cs and the concrete zoom, drop scarcity and deadline, and soften the call to action into a single question or a named next thing to watch; a hard sales close breaks the spell that made the piece work (see source-notes: t=23:45, t=30:50)
- When the user has no recorded sales calls: harvest their own comments, DMs, replies, reviews, and voice notes instead, and quote the recurring phrasings verbatim; the source matters less than the words being theirs and not yours (see source-notes: t=06:20)
- When the writing is not in English: keep clear, concise, concrete and conversational as they are, and adapt cadence to the language's own rhythm; do not use Hemingway or the fifth-grade heuristic outside English, use the read-aloud and squint tests instead, which work in any script (see source-notes: t=21:50, t=26:35)
- When a claim has no evidence behind it: either find proof that passes the deaf and mute test or cut the claim; never dress an opinion as a result (see source-notes: t=12:05, t=13:15)
- When the hook scores badly: name which of the four variables is weak and fix that one; a hook with no credibility needs a number or a named source, not stronger adjectives (see source-notes: t=28:05)
- When the user asks for many calls to action or wants to add one more ask: refuse and pick the single most valuable action; split asks split the response (see source-notes: t=31:25)
- When the draft reads corporate: read it aloud and replace every phrase nobody would say out loud, the way please do not hesitate to reach out becomes text me if you need anything (see source-notes: t=24:30, t=25:05)

## Known gotchas

- Reaching for templates and hook formulas before doing the research. Fix: Templates without the thinking leave you repeating shapes you cannot adapt. Run the 6Ps first. (see source-notes: t=02:40, t=03:10)
- Writing features instead of outcomes. Fix: Run the So What ladder until the answer is a state the reader wants to be in. (see source-notes: t=11:14)
- Trying to write more clearly by adding words. Fix: Delete what is unclear instead, starting from the end. (see source-notes: t=21:00)
- School habits: long sentences, big words, no first person, no contractions. Fix: The reader is not a teacher being paid to finish your work. Write how the reader speaks. (see source-notes: t=20:15, t=24:30)
- Uniform sentence length that drones. Fix: Vary short, medium and long. Squint at the shape of the page. (see source-notes: t=25:30, t=26:45)
- Proof that cannot be pointed at. Fix: Replace adjectives with logos, numbers, and named outcomes. (see source-notes: t=13:15)
- Case studies that do not resemble the reader. Fix: Match the proof to the avatar before using it. (see source-notes: t=12:45)
- Content that ends without asking for anything. Fix: One call to action, with who, what, when and how. (see source-notes: t=30:50)

## Output format

Open with the single biggest problem in one sentence, then the blocks that apply.

**Brief mode.** When there is no draft, say so and skip the line edit, or
keep it and relabel it as the lines this brief would default into. Then open
with the biggest problem in the brief's framing rather than in the copy.

**Slotted formats.** A landing page hero, an email, and an ad each have
several slots. Name the inventory before writing (for a hero: eyebrow, H1,
subhead, proof line, button, under-button microcopy), and say which of the
6Ps each slot carries. The three-to-five word budget is for on-screen video
text only; an H1 can run to a dozen words if the cadence earns it.

**Alternates.** One call to action, always. Headlines are different: give one
recommendation plus two or three alternates, and say what each one trades.

**Line edit.** For a draft, a table, worst line first:

```
| Their line | Test it fails | Rewrite |
```

Name the failing test by its own name: clear, concise, concrete,
conversational, or cadence. One line per row, and show the rewrite, never
just the diagnosis.

**The rewrite.** The full piece, clean and ready to use, with no annotation
inside it. Annotation goes after, never in the middle of copy the user has
to paste somewhere.

**Hook score.** When there is a hook or headline:

```
| Variable | Score /5 | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benefit | | | |
| Relevance | | | |
| Credibility | | | |
| Perceived effort | | (lower is better) | |
```

Name the weakest variable and fix only that one.

**Close.** The call to action laid out as who, what, when, how. If any of the
four is missing, say which and what it should be.

**What I assumed.** Any fact, number, or audience detail you filled in. Mark
every invented number as a placeholder in square brackets.

## Writing outside English (not shown in the video)

Clear, concise, concrete and conversational transfer unchanged. Three things
do not:

- **Hemingway and the fifth-grade heuristic are English-only.** Use the read
  aloud test and the squint test instead. Both work in any script.
- **Cadence is language-specific.** Arabic prose carries rhythm through
  clause length and connectors rather than through syllable count, and the
  same sentence rendered in Modern Standard and in dialect has a different
  pulse. Vary clause length rather than counting words, and always read it
  aloud in the register it will be spoken in.
- **Register is the whole game in diglossic languages.** In Arabic the gap
  between Modern Standard and dialect is the single loudest signal of who you
  are talking to. Pick one and hold it. Mixing reads as a translation, which
  is the same failure as corporate English.

Arabic adds one thing English does not have: grammatical gender on every
second-person address. Deciding between masculine, feminine, or a rephrase
that avoids both is an audience decision, not a style one. Ask who the
majority reader is, or write around it and say that you did.

For on-screen text in any language, the concise rule tightens: three to five
words is the working budget, and the reader gets one pass at reading speed,
not yours.

## When there is nothing to sell (not shown in the video)

The course assumes an offer. Emotional, artistic and personal work has none,
and a four-part call to action at the end of a piece that earned its silence
will break it. In that case:

- Keep clear, concise, concrete, conversational and cadence. They are what
  makes the piece land at all, and the concrete zoom is the single most
  useful tool in the whole course for feeling.
- Drop scarcity and deadline. Nothing is running out.
- Keep cost of inaction only where it is honest, and state it as recognition
  rather than threat.
- Soften the close to one of three: a question the reader answers in their
  own head, a question they answer in the comments, or a named next piece.
  One of them, not several.

The rest of the method still applies. The empathy map and the verbatim
harvest are more valuable here, not less, because recognition is the entire
product.

## Honesty boundary (not shown in the video)

Two techniques here cut both ways and the course sets no limit, so this one
does.

**Describing someone's pain better than they can** earns trust because it
implies understanding. It becomes manipulation the moment the understanding
is fake, meaning you named the pain accurately and cannot actually relieve
it. Name pain you can answer. If you cannot answer it, you are writing
recognition, not an offer, and the close must change accordingly.

**Quoting the audience's own words back** is research when the words are
real and paraphrased faithfully, and it is fabrication when you invent a
quote and present it as a customer's. When there is no audience language available at all, say so plainly, mark
the draft's register as inferred, and give the user the twenty-minute
instruction for collecting it rather than writing in a voice you invented and
presenting it as theirs. A named weakness beats a fabricated strength.

Harvest real language. Never attribute
an invented sentence to a real person, never publish a customer's words
without permission, and never sharpen a quote into something they did not
say.

Everything else follows the rules already in this repo: claim only what the
proof supports, mark borrowed proof as borrowed, and never round a number
in the direction that flatters you.

## Choosing between two drafts (not shown in the video)

The course improves one draft and never compares two. When there are two,
score both on the five tests plus the hook formula, then break ties in this
order: the one whose promise is a state rather than a feature, then the one
whose proof can be pointed at, then the one that reads better aloud. Length
is not a tiebreaker; the shorter one wins only if nothing was lost.

## Worked example

Before, a real conference talk description: "In this session we will explore
best practices and actionable insights for leveraging modern tooling to
optimise your engineering workflows and unlock team productivity."

- Clear: fails. After reading it you cannot say what the talk is about.
- Concise: fails. Twenty-four words, and "best practices", "actionable
  insights", "leverage", "optimise" and "unlock" carry no information.
- Concrete: fails. Nothing can be pictured. No tool, no workflow, no team.
- Conversational: fails. Nobody describes their own talk this way out loud.
- Cadence: fails. One long clause with no break in it.
- Proof: none. The deaf and mute test finds nothing to point at.
- Priority and CTA: absent. There is no reason to attend rather than read the
  slides later.

After: "We deleted 60% of our CI pipeline and builds got faster. I will show
you the four jobs we cut, why we were wrong about two of them, and how to
find yours. Bring your own pipeline; we will look at one live at the end."

Short, medium, long. Every claim points at a number or an action. The reader
can picture the thing, knows who it is for, and knows what happens if they
turn up. The percentage is a placeholder until the speaker confirms it.
