# Hook Writer

Given a topic, target viewer, and format, produce several candidate hooks that follow the three-step formula, with on-screen text and a first hit of value, and explain why each line does its job.

Learned from: How to Create Irresistible Hooks (and blow up your content) (https://youtu.be/LmXpbP7dD48). The timestamped beat sheet is
in `references/source-notes.md`; anything copied from the screen is in
`references/verbatim-assets.md`.

## Related skill

The hook covers only the opening lines. When the user also needs the body of
the piece to hold attention, or shares a full script, use `story-loop-writer`
(stakes, big question, head fake, re-hook) for everything after the hook.
The snapback of a hook is a natural first big question for the loop. Use
`algorithm-strategist` before either when the user is still deciding what to
make, for whom, or why past videos did not get views; it fixes the avatar,
topic band, and stance that the hook then has to serve.

`brand-idea-miner` decides the idea itself (topic, angle, visual format,
proof plan) before any hook is written; when the user has no idea yet, start
there. Its collision (the outside-niche frame that makes the angle
non-obvious) is usually the strongest lever for the lean.

## Prerequisites

- A topic, the target viewer, and the format (short-form vertical, YouTube long-form, or written) from the user. Ask for the target viewer if it is missing; the lean-in lever depends on it.

## Procedure

1. **Pin down topic clarity and pick a lean-in lever for lines 1 and 2 (the context lean).** The first two lines must let the right viewer self-select in and make them lean forward. The four levers are common ground, a benefit or pain point, a metaphor that simplifies, or a fact that blows their mind. Tricking the wrong viewer in is a loss, not a win. (see source-notes: t=01:17, t=01:45, t=02:05)
2. **Write the scroll stop interjection: one contrast line that stops the reader without paying off.** It works like a stun gun. Start it with 'but' (or however, yet, although, on the other hand). It is only a setup for the next line, so it should raise the question, not answer it. (see source-notes: t=02:46, t=03:15)
3. **Write the contrarian snapback: a claim that reverses the direction of the lean while staying on topic.** The bigger the reversal, the stronger the hook. Abstractly the whole hook reads 'if you want X, don't do Y, do Z', and Z has to be something the rest of the content genuinely delivers, or the viewer churns and the creator looks like a fraud. (see source-notes: t=03:56, t=04:12, t=08:05)
4. **Reframe the lean around a desire or pain the viewer already has, and anchor unfamiliar ideas to something known.** People accept new perspectives on things they already care about. 'If you want better sleep, take magnesium' beats 'magnesium is one of 21 core minerals'. For complex or niche topics, wrap the unknown in a known brand, celebrity, movement, or cultural reference (cult hopping) so the viewer feels comfortable rather than dumb. The known anchor can also be the viewer's own current tool, language, or community (Python for a Python audience, the famous V60 recipe for coffee nerds); what matters is that it is already trusted. (see source-notes: t=11:36, t=11:50, t=12:33, t=13:05)
5. **Distill the context lean into three to five bold on-screen words and suggest an opening visual with calibrated motion.** Text on screen is read faster than speech and is described as roughly 100 times more powerful than spoken hooks alone. Choose words that cannot be misread (the floor-plans example used 'future of home design' rather than the product's name). The visual needs enough motion to catch the eye, not so much it confuses. (see source-notes: t=09:31, t=09:50, t=10:05, t=10:30)
6. **Place a first hit of value inside or right after the hook.** The viewer's timer runs about 4 seconds on short-form and 1 to 2 minutes on YouTube. Anything after they leave is a black screen. Front-load, then alternate context and value to earn 30 seconds at a time. (see source-notes: t=13:50, t=14:05, t=14:40)
7. **Rewrite every hook sentence short and staccato, then present 3 to 5 candidates with a line-by-line annotation.** Short sentences force clarity and raise value per word where time is scarcest. Annotate each candidate so the user can see which line is the lean, the stop, and the snap, and can swap parts between candidates. (see source-notes: t=15:04, t=15:25)

## Decision rules

- When the user gives a topic but no target viewer: ask who should self-select in. If you cannot ask, or the user's phrasing implies the viewer (as in 'as a Python developer'), state the assumption at the top of the output and proceed; the lean-in lever cannot be chosen without it (see source-notes: t=01:30)
- When the topic is niche, technical, or B2B (finance, real estate, tax, health): lead with the benefit or pain and use cult hopping; the formula still applies, as the mortgage example shows (see source-notes: t=05:10, t=12:33)
- When the snapback promises something the script does not deliver: either change the snapback or flag that the body needs to deliver Z; never leave an unbacked promise (see source-notes: t=08:15)
- When the format is short-form vertical: the viewer's timer is about 4 seconds, so the on-screen text and line 1 must land the topic in the first second and the first value hit must arrive by about the 4 second mark; the full three lines can run 6 to 10 seconds spoken fast. Supply 3 to 5 word title text (see source-notes: t=13:55, t=09:50)
- When the user just says 'YouTube': treat it as long-form unless they mention Shorts, vertical, or under a minute; long-form phrasing like 'welcome back to the channel' is a tell
- When the format is a YouTube long-form video: the hook can run longer but a first hit of value must land within 1 to 2 minutes (see source-notes: t=14:00)
- When asked to critique an existing hook: map its lines to lean, stop, snap; name which parts are missing; check for on-screen text, a value hit, and sentence length; then propose a rewrite (see source-notes: t=05:54, t=08:05)

## Known gotchas

- A list of proven hook templates without the psychology produces hooks that sound viral but do not fit the viewer. Fix: Always derive the lean from the target viewer's desire or pain rather than from a template. (see source-notes: t=00:05, t=07:10)
- Topic clarity gets sacrificed for cleverness, so the wrong viewer clicks in and churns. Fix: Line 1 or 2 must name the topic plainly enough that the target viewer knows this is for them. (see source-notes: t=01:25)
- On-screen text uses the product's obscure name and gets misunderstood. Fix: Use plain descriptive words in the title text; save the name for the body. (see source-notes: t=10:15)
- Too much motion in the opening visual overwhelms; too little bores. Fix: Suggest one clear subject with modest movement, like leaning toward the camera. (see source-notes: t=10:35)

## Output format

Deliver 3 to 5 candidates unless the user asks for a different number. For each:

```
### Candidate N: <two-word label>
Lean:  <line 1 and 2>
Stop:  <the "but" line>
Snap:  <the reversal>
On-screen text: <3 to 5 words>
Opening visual: <one sentence, with the motion described>
First value hit: <the concrete thing the viewer learns in the next sentence>
Why it works: <one or two sentences naming the lever and the size of the reversal>
```

Add one line per candidate saying whether the snap teases Z or reveals it.

Close with a short recommendation of which candidate to shoot first and why,
using the rubric below.

When critiquing an existing hook, open with this table before the rewrite:

```
| Their line | What it is doing | Slot (lean / stop / snap / none) |
```

followed by a ranked list of what is missing, worst first. Then give the
rewrite as candidates in the format above.

When the user has not shared the body of the content, write value hits as
illustrative placeholders in square brackets, shaped like the real thing, and
say plainly that any snap the body cannot deliver must be changed. Illustrative
beats generic because the user sees the shape they must fill.

## Choosing between candidates (not shown in the video)

The video gives no scoring method, so use this rubric, which is derived from
the checks the presenter applies to his own examples:

| Check | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Topic clarity | Would the target viewer know in line 1 or 2 that this is for them? | t=01:25 |
| Lean strength | Does the lean use a real desire or pain of that viewer, or just a template? | t=11:36 |
| Stop | Is there one contrast line that raises a question without answering it? | t=02:46 |
| Reversal size | How far does the snap move from the direction of the lean? Bigger is stronger. | t=04:00 |
| Believable Z | Can the body of the content actually deliver what the snap promises? | t=08:15 |
| Speed | Spoken in about 4 seconds for short-form; value hit within 1 to 2 minutes for long-form | t=13:55 |
| Density | Are the sentences short and staccato? | t=15:04 |

Prefer the candidate that wins on believable Z and reversal size. A dazzling
snap the content cannot back up is worse than a modest one it can. Reversal
size is a judgment call; describe it as small, medium, or large and say what
the viewer expected versus what the snap delivers.

When the user asks for "the" on-screen text or overlay, give one recommended
line paired with the candidate you recommend, then note that each candidate
carries its own.

## Writing the first value hit (not shown in the video)

The video says to front-load value but not how to pick it. Take the single
most surprising, immediately usable fact or step from the body of the content
and state it right after the snap in one sentence. If the user has not shared
the body, ask for the one thing they most want the viewer to take away, or
mark the value hit as a placeholder they must fill.

## Tease or reveal the snap (not shown in the video)

The Sphere and mortgage examples tease Z ("the most impressive part is the
audio", "the biggest waste is actually coming from...") while the video's own
opening reveals it ("understand the psychology"). Teasing suits entertainment
and story-led content where the payoff is the whole video. Revealing suits
educational content where the viewer needs to trust they will learn something
specific. Offer at least one of each when the format allows.

## Beyond video (not shown in the video)

The presenter says the formula applies "in conversation, in life" but shows
only video. For written posts, emails, and thumbnails, keep the same three
beats: the first sentence or headline is the lean, the second is the stop, the
third is the snap. Replace the on-screen text with the headline or subject
line and the opening visual with the thumbnail or cover image.

## Claims about real people, brands, and professional advice (not shown in the video)

Cult hopping borrows credibility from real names, and pain-led leans in
finance, health, tax, or legal topics make factual claims. Keep the borrowed
name to a comparison or metaphor rather than a claim about what that person
did, and phrase professional-advice snaps so they stay defensible ("one of the
first things a lawyer looks for" rather than "guarantees you lose"). Suggest a
disclaimer in the body where the niche calls for one. A hook the creator has
to retract is worse than a slightly softer one.

## Worked example

From the source at t=04:12, annotated:

```
Lean:  The tech in the Vegas Sphere is insane. Biggest screen ever built, 20 times bigger than an IMAX.
       (topic clarity: the Sphere and its tech; lever: mind-blowing fact)
Stop:  But get this, the screen is actually the least impressive part of the whole thing.
       (contrast word "but"; contradicts what the viewer can see)
Snap:  Because the most impressive part is the audio. This is going to blow your mind.
       (reverses from screen to audio, still on topic; teases Z)
```
