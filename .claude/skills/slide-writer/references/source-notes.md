## Agreed across videos (the spine)

0. **Match the document to the occasion first.** Keynote, informal discussion,
   formal read-ahead, board, external, documentation each need a different
   density. V17 02:00, V21 03:00 and 06:30, V23 01:00.
1. **The storyline lives in the action titles.** Read only the titles and you have
   the whole argument. V01 00:00 to 03:00, V05 06:30, V07 02:00, V09 05:00, V14
   02:30, V15 00:30, V16 08:30, V19 01:30.
2. **One message per slide, decided before any layout.** V04 13:15, V15 00:30, V19
   03:30.
3. **Every slide is a table.** Types of information in columns, items in rows. No
   clotheslines. V02 04:45, V06 01:00, V15 04:00, V16 07:00, V18 01:00 to 04:15.
4. **Chart with implications** (chart left, so-what right) is the default
   quantitative page. V02 17:15, V06 07:15, V09 10:30.
5. **One body font size per page**, title and footnotes excepted. V02 08:00, V05
   02:45, V14 03:30.
6. **Colour carries meaning or it goes.** Grey everything, accent the point; a
   legend for any coding. V02 18:30, V04 01:30, V05 03:30, V09 04:15, V14 05:00.
7. **Data-to-ink.** Strip gridlines, connector lines and redundant axes; label the
   bars. V04 04:00, V14 04:00.
8. **Alignment and margins.** The action title's left edge and the footnote's
   right edge bound the page; nothing jitters between pages. V02 08:45, V05 03:45,
   V14 06:30, V20 entire.
9. **Process:** messaging, structure, graphics, supporting text, polishing. V15 and
   V19 give the identical five steps independently.
10. **No decoration that does not inform:** no SmartArt (V08), no PowerPoint chart
    templates with gloss and 3D (V04 12:00), no animations (V05 11:15, V15 11:45),
    no background fills in charts (V14 05:00).

## Single-source points worth keeping

- The action-title ladder and its anti-patterns: general truths, absolutes,
  process instead of results, more than two lines (V01).
- Sketch the whole deck on paper and align with the lead before building: the
  "Mickey Mouse master" or strawman (V07, V19).
- Integrated slides: build the chart into the table to save the reader the jump
  (V06 09:15).
- Facts, summary, synthesis; data telling versus data storytelling; same data,
  different story by audience (V09).
- Every page self-explanatory when pulled out: chart title, unit, percent of what,
  date, legend, source, survey geography (V23).
- Situation, complication (ending in a question), solution for introductions and
  executive summaries (V13).
- What AI-built slides get wrong (V10): bars into the footer, misaligned to the
  title, cramped bullets, wrong dash, layout elements copied without content,
  inverted colour hierarchy, parallel items with different sub-structures.
- Corporate-deck review: 16:9 not 4:3, ISO currency codes, consistent negative
  notation, no unexplained "+7% / +9% FX-adjusted" (V14).
- Icons sparingly, never without text, best as recurring markers (V11).
- Text boxes have default internal margins that break alignment; zero them and
  set as default (V12, V20).
- Selective bolding; link title numbers to the source so they never go stale (V15).
- Room and equipment preparation, 30 minutes early (V22).

## Tensions, and how the skill settles them

| Tension | Sides | Skill's rule |
|---|---|---|
| One font size, always? | V05 and V14 strict; V02 10:45 allows a 40pt hero number | Strict by default. One deliberate exception per page, for a single headline number. |
| Chart plus bullet column, or integrated? | V06 07:15 calls chart-plus-implications "the mother of all layouts"; V06 09:15 says integrated is better because it removes the jump | Simple chart: chart plus implications. Complex chart, or bullets that each refer to a part of it: integrate. |
| Number circles | V15 06:30 says a real chart is usually better; V06 11:00 uses circles as an added dimension | Circles only as a secondary dimension inside an integrated layout, never instead of a chart. |
| Legend required? | V05 03:30 says every colour needs a meaning or a legend; V23 07:30 omits it when the title explains the highlight | Legend unless the action title makes the highlight self-evident. |
| Text-heavy pages | V05 says de-word; V06 03:30 shows text-heavy measure tables; V11 says some text pages are fine | Main deck lean; text-heavy pages go to the appendix. |
| Full pages or minimal pages? | V17 06:30: read-ahead decks need more text to stand alone; V21 11:00: for boards keep language basic and fonts large; V17 02:30: keynotes are a picture and a few words | Decide by who reads it and whether you are there. Presented to a board: simple words, larger type, the detail in the appendix. Circulated without you: enough text to stand alone. Documentation: the most text. Keynote: almost none. |
| Banking versus consulting style | V05 12:15 | Consulting style for boardroom and leadership audiences; banking conventions only for banking audiences. |

## Gaps the videos leave

- Choosing a storyline structure for the whole deck beyond SCS (Pyramid grouping,

- Building the actual file. The videos are PowerPoint-by-hand; the skill must work

- Presenting live versus a read-ahead document.

# V01 How to create Storylines (action titles), 13:18
- 00:00 action titles are the single most important element of a business slide.
- 01:00 storyline lives in the titles: read only the titles start to end and you get every key message ("horizontal logic"). 02:30 the slide body is the "vertical logic": a reader stops on an interesting title and reads the page.
- 01:30 why: C-level readers receive 50 to 100 page decks every week and cannot read them; they skim titles.
- 03:00 definition: a header that summarises the slide's content as a complete sentence.
- 03:30 test it with "so what?": what is the implication for the problem at hand. 04:30 if you cannot find the so-what, the slide goes to the appendix or out.
- 05:00 avoid general truths ("managing risk is important for this project's success").
- 05:30 avoid absolutes (all, never, always); one counterexample from a board member and the message is dead.
- 06:30 quantify whenever numbers exist.
- 06:45 results, not process ("when conducting analysis and interviews we learned a lot" is process). Top-down communication.
- 07:30 concise; never more than two lines.
- 08:00 exercise, market-sizing slide for North America and Asia Pacific, the title ladder:
  "Derive potentials for an expansion" (topic title, not an action title) →
  "Expanding to new geographies creates a positive EBIT potential" (generic) →
  "Expansion to North America and Asia Pacific creates a positive EBIT potential" →
  "... of 22 EUR million" (quantified) →
  "Expansion to North America and Asia Pacific with 22 EUR million EBIT potential" (concise, the target).
- 11:30 telegraphic style is fine: drop articles and filler words.

# V02 Ex-McKinsey creates a slide presentation with you (live build), 27:23
Example deck: a sponsorship pitch for his channel (intro, content overview, pilot results, packages and pricing, contact).
- 01:30 first step is always the storyline: write the action titles of every page before building any page; jot the planned contents as bullets under each.
- 04:00 title page: document name, then subtitle with date and document type ("discussion document").
- 04:45 core principle "every page is a table": structure every slide in columns and rows.
- 05:30 column headers: a text element plus a line under it, grouped (Ctrl+G); left column "Audience", right "Introduction".
- 06:30 several items inside a column become rows.
- 07:00 Shift+Ctrl+drag copies an element while locking the axis.
- 07:45 give repeated elements identical widths.
- 08:00 one font size for all text on a page, except the action title and the footnote (example 16pt). 10:45 small, deliberate exceptions allowed (a 40pt key number).
- 08:30 align with the align buttons; he moves the quick-access toolbar below the ribbon to shorten mouse travel.
- 08:45 margins: the action title's left edge and the footnote's right edge are the page boundary; nothing crosses them.
- 10:00 hierarchy by colour: column headers bold black; emphasis inside columns bold dark blue.
- 11:00 icons from a licensed library to avoid copyright problems (he sponsors one), recoloured to the deck colour.
- 12:30 separator lines between rows: light grey, dashed; a solid, heavier line to separate major sections.
- 13:00 group, then distribute evenly.
- 16:00 secondary information in the header unbolded and grey.
- 16:30 image grids (12 thumbnails as 3 rows by 4 columns), aligned to the boundaries, with picture borders.
- 17:15 chart page blueprint: chart in the left column, conclusions in the right column, each with a column header.
- 18:00 think-cell for charts (common in consulting); PowerPoint's own charts work too.
- 18:30 colour sparingly: chart in grey, only the point being made in the accent colour.
- 20:00 options page: three packages as three columns.
- 20:45 the "clothesline": a long, unstructured list of bullets. Group bullets into logical rows instead.
- 21:30 boxes: light-blue fill, dark-blue border; Arial as the base font.
- 22:00 a repeated set (channels, regions, products) always appears in the same order throughout the deck, with the same icons.
- 23:00 an arrow spanning all columns for something common to all options; a white box behind it to clean up.
- 24:00 a stray grouped element breaks distribution; ungroup and regroup.
- 24:30 commercials row at the bottom.
- 25:30 contact page: simple, not over-styled.

# V04 Data Visualization for Slide Presentations, 14:56 (sponsored by think-cell)
- 01:30 colours must carry meaning; otherwise tone down to basic colours. 02:00 put a single accent on the point you are making (the latest year in a time series).
- 02:30 remove connector lines between bars, background gridlines, and the y-axis when values sit on top of the bars. 03:00 lighten a heavy black x-axis.
- 03:15 add a difference arrow that carries the message (revenue +400% from 2015 to 2021). 04:00 that number can be cited in the action title.
- 04:00 data-to-ink ratio: as much data as possible with as little ink as possible; delete every helper line that does not serve the message.
- 05:00 shares of a total: a pie chart gets messy past about five slices, and people read areas badly; a sorted bar chart compares relative sizes more intuitively.
- 06:00 choose the chart type deliberately at the start. 06:30 his cheat sheet: component comparison → pie or stacked column; absolute comparison → bar or waterfall; time series → line or column; also distributions and correlations.
- 07:30 three numeric dimensions (marketing spend, unit price, volume) → bubble chart; add a trend line to make a correlation salient.
- 10:45 P&L → waterfall chart, with totals and subtotals; stack segments within a bar.
- 11:30 round long numbers (show in millions) rather than printing raw figures.
- 12:00 PowerPoint's built-in chart templates (gloss, shadow, 3D, shaded backgrounds, surface charts) look unprofessional; avoid them.
- 13:15 one message per slide; several charts on one page only when all are needed for that one message, otherwise split them.

# V05 Consulting Presentation Tips (reviewing viewer slides), 15:24
- 00:00 "slide writing": creating presentations in the style of MBB (McKinsey, BCG, Bain). He taught it at McKinsey for six years.
- 02:00 mixed font sizes are the main cause of a messy page (one slide had 28, 24, 20, 18 and 12). 02:45 rule: the whole body is one font size; only the title and footnotes differ.
- 03:00 dotted lines should be light and in the background, never the heaviest line on the chart.
- 03:30 every colour either has a direct meaning or there is a legend.
- 03:45 no "jitter": the title, body and footer must sit in exactly the same position on every page. Flip through the PDF and nothing should move.
- 05:00 separator lines make a list of elements look finished.
- 05:15 give a group of elements a subtitle naming its organising principle ("competitive advantages").
- 06:00 every percentage says "percent of what": market share by units or by dollars.
- 06:30 a header such as "Market and industry" is not an action title; without one the message is unreadable.
- 07:30 never paste a chart as a screenshot; rebuild it natively.
- 07:45 introduce terms before using them (which industry, user penetration of what); say what a chart means (is a low number good or bad?).
- 08:45 too much text: nobody reads a page of full sentences, even as a handout. "De-word": bullets instead of sentences, drop articles. Example: "The value of this information depends on the trust within the network" → "Information value depends on network trust".
- 11:15 animations and heavy graphics distract and read as unprofessional at C-level; consulting decks usually have none, partly because they are often printed and read rather than presented.
- 12:15 banking decks carry far more text, and their "football field" valuation charts confuse anyone outside banking. For boardroom audiences the consulting style works better.
- 14:00 "there is no law of slide presentations": these are his opinions.

# V06 Effective Slide Blueprints, 12:57 (sponsored by think-cell)
- 00:00 rely on a few layout blueprints so a 20 to 30 page deck does not need each slide designed from scratch.
- 01:00 key principle: every slide is a table, a collection of rows and columns (not an inserted PowerPoint table).
- 01:45 three slide types: qualitative, quantitative, integrated.
- 02:15 qualitative blueprint: different *types* of information go in different columns (principle, example, recommendation); the items they apply to go in rows. Mixing types in one bullet list is the common error; avoid "clotheslines".
- 03:30 example: Measures | Rationale. Text-heavy versions belong in the appendix.
- 04:00 richer example: Recommendation | Details | EBIT impact (number) | Roadmap/timing.
- 04:45 the column headers can also be a timeline or process chevrons.
- 07:15 "the mother of all quantitative layouts": Methodology | Chart | Implications (the so-what). About half of all his consulting pages followed a version of it; often the methodology column is dropped to give the chart more room.
- 08:15 example: bubble chart (market size, growth, margin by region) with a key-insights column.
- 08:45 when the chart is self-explanatory, drop the right column and add a callout: a text box with two short lines pointing to the element.
- 09:15 integrated slides, very common in MBB and rare elsewhere: build the chart into the table so the reader does not have to jump between bullets and a separate chart (that jump costs cognitive effort).
- 10:30 example: label | bar chart | details | strategic assessment, where a callout is simply another column.
- 11:00 example: waterfall | an extra numeric dimension as circles | implications, with shading on the part of the chart that the right column discusses.

# V07 How Consultants Make Slides (the Mickey Mouse master), 8:04
- 01:00 the "Mickey Mouse master", also called a strawman: a paper draft of the whole presentation, usually made by the project lead or partner, then handed to an associate to build. He was given one in his first weeks at McKinsey.
- 02:00 draw a grid; each square is one page. Write a brief version of each action title (it need not be final) and a rough sketch of the chart type.
- 02:45 worked storyline for a company under cost pressure: (1) "Company is in market with x% growth" (bar chart with CAGR); (2) "Market share of company is decreasing due to competition" (100% bars over time); (3) profitability will fall by 200,000 (waterfall 2020 to 2021); (4) three areas to improve; (5) backup pages for each of the three; (6) project timeline to end of 2021 (Gantt). Situation, then complication, then the answer, then proof and plan.
- 05:15 it works in reverse too: an associate sketches the deck and walks the lead through the flow before building anything. It takes minutes, so a complete change of storyline costs nothing.

# V08 Avoid SmartArt in PowerPoint, 20:25
- 03:00 SmartArt shows up in amateur decks and is essentially never used in consulting or large-company presentations; a partner will tell you to remove it.
- 04:00 the typical case is a process chart (steps with bullets underneath), which he built hundreds of times at McKinsey.
- 05:30 SmartArt designs are playful rather than practical; the plain "header plus text per step" layout barely exists.
- 06:30 changing one font size resizes the whole object; pasted text shrinks by itself; boxes cannot be resized independently. Fighting it takes longer than building by hand.
- 11:45 build it from basic shapes instead: one flat chevron or box, duplicate it (Ctrl+Shift+drag), distribute horizontally, add text boxes aligned left and distributed, separators as 1pt dotted lines.
- 14:00 complex shapes come from combining basic shapes with Merge Shapes. 16:30 order matters for Subtract: select the shape to keep first, then the cutter. Also Union, Combine, Fragment, Intersect.

# V09 Data Storytelling: Turn Data into Decisions, 14:29 (sponsored by think-cell)
- 01:00 three levels of insight: facts (raw data), summary (condensed restatement of the facts), synthesis (what it means, the implications). 01:45 inexperienced people stop at the summary and leave the reader to work out the meaning.
- 02:15 Brent Dykes: "data telling" (repeating facts, the summary) versus "data storytelling" (fitting facts into a recommendation and narrative, the synthesis).
- 02:45 storytelling with data needs three things: visualisation, narrative, and an understanding of the audience.
- 03:15 example, browser market share over time: a 100% stacked column with unreadable labels → a line chart with toned-down colours and a chart title.
- 04:15 then decide what the story is. Same data, two stories: (a) Chrome's rise, with Chrome bold, in an accent colour and with its logo; (b) for an audience of Apple users, "Safari held about 20% despite Chrome", with Safari highlighted instead. The action title states whichever story you chose.
- 08:30 a slide combining an analysis and a chart "looks like a collage". Fix: a strong action title (growth plus share from acquisitions), a simple bar chart, numbers rounded to millions, toned-down colours, a difference arrow for organic growth, the growth figure reused in the title, and a right column of implications that connect to each other rather than random points.
- 11:15 a three-chart slide: left-align everything; a proper legend with left-aligned labels; toned-down colours with shading on the focus segment (Millennials). 12:15 when one chart mixes two category systems (generations and income brackets), explain both with small boxes. 12:45 positive versus negative in distinct toned-down colours. 13:00 re-aggregate data into the same segmentation used across the rest of the deck (Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z).

# V10 Ex-McKinsey tests Claude for PowerPoint, 15:04 (filmed about a month after a February release, i.e. early 2026)
- 00:30 his view: LLM slide tools have long fallen well short of consulting quality.
- 01:00 Copilot's "new slide" feature has an 800-character prompt limit; the result had large white space, no proper title element, random stock photos, colours not matching the template, black headers.
- 02:30 Claude for PowerPoint caveats: beta, needs a paid plan, needs a live internet connection.
- 03:30 it first inspects the existing slides to infer content and style, and reports the colour palette it found. Slower than Copilot, but he notes the fair comparison is how long a human would take.
- 05:15 defects in its first slide, his checklist of what AI gets wrong:
  - an accent bar running into the footer area;
  - a bar not aligned with where the action title starts;
  - bullets without spacing between the bullet and the text;
  - the wrong dash used for a range of weeks;
  - a layout element copied from a template slide without the content that justified it;
  - an inverted colour hierarchy: darker colours should mark higher levels, so title-style elements darker than subtitle-style ones.
- 07:30 content defects: three parallel programmes were each described with different sub-headings (format/target group/benefits vs duration/delivery vs audience/goal/approach). Parallel items need identical structure, possibly with a legend of row buckets on the left so they can be compared.
- 08:30 written feedback on these defects was mostly not fixed.
- 09:15 he then gave it a markdown file of best practices and about five consulting-firm decks as style references. 10:30 no memory across sessions, so this must be provided every session.
- 12:45 reordering slides while it works confused it.
- 13:15 result: no better, arguably worse; the action title was not in the template's style, the footer still wrong, the font size increased.
- 14:00 verdict: improving fast but not yet usable for production consulting slides.

# V11 How to use Icons for Slide Presentations, 10:03 (sponsored by an icon library)
- 00:00 "look and feel": text-only pages read as a wall of text even when correct. Some text pages are fine; not every page.
- 02:00 consulting firms have icon libraries; Google image search is mostly copyrighted and risks your employer. Use a licensed library.
- 04:30 make room on the left of each row and place an icon per bucket (country flags, data analysis, express delivery, mining tools), recoloured to the deck's dark blue, then align.
- 05:45 vector icons scale without losing resolution.
- 06:30 any set of distinct buckets is an opportunity for icons.
- 06:45 never icons without explanatory text; icons are rarely self-explanatory and people read them differently.
- 07:30 best use: elements that repeat through the deck (levers, opportunities). Establish the icon on a structure page and reuse it, so the reader connects pages.
- 08:00 do not spam icons; on every page they become meaningless, repetitive and childish.

# V12 Fix your Text Boxes, 10:02
- 00:30 by default PowerPoint inserts text boxes with internal margins on all four sides (and in Calibri when the deck is in Arial).

# V13 Business Documents: Situation, Complication, Solution, 13:44
- 00:00 the introduction is the most important part of a business document; it must capture interest. SCS is taught in nearly every MBB onboarding programme.
- 01:45 situation, complication, solution (beginning, middle, end).
- 02:00 situation: a few sentences everyone agrees with; uncontroversial common ground, yet interesting enough to make them want more. Examples: "The profit margin of our company has been steadily declining over the last five years"; "YouTube channels have a great capacity to teach educational material".
- 02:30 length scales with the document: a paragraph or two for a 2 to 3 page memo, a page or two for a 50 to 100 page document.
- 05:45 complication: adds complexity, may be more controversial, and need not be a problem. From The Pyramid Principle (Minto): something went wrong; something could go wrong; something changed or could change; here is what you might expect to find; someone has a different point of view; there are three alternatives.
- 08:15 the complication ends in a question, the most logical next question the reader would ask (what is their view? what are the options? how is it changing?).
- 09:00 solution: a concise answer to that question, or simply the key message. It starts the top-down structure of the document.
- 10:00 the solution also outlines the document's structure, but in ideas, not in section names: not "the document also outlines enablers" but half a sentence naming the most important enabler.
- 11:00 worked example: "The profit margins of our company have been steadily declining over the last five years. In addition, new competitors have entered our home market this year, putting additional pressure on our market share. What can we do to address this issue? This report outlines how a shift from product selling to solution selling could defend our market share and increase our profitability. We will highlight what processes and capabilities we need to make this happen and how the implementation roadmap looks." The complication also answers "why act now".

# V14 Ex-McKinsey reviews corporate slides (an Amazon earnings-call deck), 11:39
- 01:30 the biggest problem is purpose: an earnings deck for investors that is "just a data dump", with no narrative. A falling free-cash-flow chart with no explanation, driver or plan erodes investor confidence. Fix with action titles and a storyline.
- 03:00 aspect ratio 4:3 is outdated; use 16:9 (more space, no black bars).
- 03:30 mixed font sizes again; double dashes with no meaning look unprofessional.
- 04:00 data-to-ink: drop the y-axis when values sit on the bars; label every bar, not just the first and last (otherwise the reader needs a ruler).
- 05:00 no decorative background fills in charts; they waste ink and can make bars vanish on a black-and-white printout.
- 05:30 negative numbers in one consistent notation (a minus sign, or brackets, never both).
- 06:15 currency as a three-letter code (USD), not "$", because many currencies use the dollar sign.
- 06:30 alignment: as few alignment lines on the page as possible; every box, chart axis, legend and footnote snaps to the page boundaries unless there is a hierarchical reason. A unit label should be centred or left-aligned to its axis, not floating. MBB will give feedback on this.
- 08:00 a takeaway box should be centred, and should say something: "long-term goal: optimise free cash flow" with the number falling says nothing.
- 09:00 ambiguous labels: "+7% YoY, +9% FX-adjusted" without saying which one the bars show forces the reader to do arithmetic.
- 09:45 a pie chart beside a bar chart with no visible link, and a pie colour that matches a bar colour, implies a false relationship.

# V15 Create Slides like McKinsey and BCG (step-by-step process), 14:05 (sponsored by think-cell)
The five-step slide creation process: messaging, structure, data and graphics, supporting text, polishing (13:15 summary).
- 00:30 step 1, messaging: be clear on what the slide says before arranging anything; the common mistake is starting layout first. One key message per slide (overview and structure pages excepted). The action title carries it.
- 01:30 worked example: an analyst briefing lists three initiatives, each with an EBIT potential. Title: "Launching subscription-based pricing, expanding into the European market and offering corporate consulting services would entail an EBIT potential of 61 EUR million."
- 02:45 step 2, structure, at two levels: the deck (an agenda that stays in sync across the document; moving a section moves its slides) and the slide (always a table layout of rows and columns; do not invent your own arrangements). Reuse your own past slides from a library and adapt them to the template.
- 06:00 step 3, data and graphics: number bubbles work sometimes, but a real chart is usually better. His chart guide: every chart is a comparison; here an absolute comparison, so a waterfall. Toned-down colours, highlight the total; link to Excel when useful; add annotations sparingly.
- 10:00 step 4, supporting text: selective bolding, a few words per bullet that carry its essence, so the reader can skim. Link numbers in text (especially in the action title) to the source sheet, because titles go stale when the analysis changes.
- 11:45 step 5, polishing: run a quality checklist, especially alignment; remove stray digital ink, transitions and animations, and invisible or off-slide content. Send single pages when needed, and attach a PDF for leaders reading on an iPad.

# V16 Create Charts like Consulting Firms, 9:59
Worked example: a bubble chart of geographic business units (x: CAGR, y: operating margin, bubble: market size).
- 01:30 three dimensions → x, y and bubble size.
- 02:00 the messy version colours every bubble differently; colour must carry meaning, otherwise one unified colour.
- 02:45 the meaning here: Asia Pacific and North America (high margin, fast growth, large) are the focus, so colour only those two in the accent blue.
- 03:30 label all three dimensions. 04:30 "percent of what": CAGR as % of revenue, operating margin as % of revenue (other margin definitions exist).
- 05:15 every chart gets a chart title (a text box plus a line), e.g. "Key metrics for geographical business units", with qualifiers such as the source ("based on financial reporting") and the year.
- 06:30 never leave the reader alone with a chart: add the so-what. Every slide is a table, so a second column on the right for implications.
- 07:45 bold the most important words of each bullet, in the deck's main colour rather than black.
- 08:30 action title with a number: "Company should further focus on Asia Pacific and North America addressing total market of 428 EUR million". A page with data should almost always have a number in its title.

# V17 What is special about Consulting Presentations?, 9:54
Answers the recurring objection "aren't these slides far too full?"
- 02:00 three styles of business presentation:
  1. "Steve Jobs" keynote: large stage, a picture and a few words per slide. 02:30 it requires a speaker; sent around on its own it is hard to understand.
  2. 03:45 informal meeting (a one-to-one with the team lead, a brainstorm): a short discussion document, used once or twice. 04:30 do not spend time on perfect layout; keep it short and simple.
  3. 05:00 consulting style: formal audiences (senior management, board), progress reports. 05:30 often not presented at all but handed out, then forwarded around the organisation to people who were not in the room.
- 06:00 anecdote: a BCG deck still circulated and referred to as "the BCG document" years after the project.
- 06:30 so every page must be self-explanatory, which needs more detail and more text than a keynote.
- 07:00 the author's reputation is tied to the document for its whole shelf life; errors and imprecise statements are remembered.
- 07:30 teams change; a year later nobody may be left to ask what the numbers meant.
- 08:30 this applies to anyone in corporate or banking presenting to C-level or senior management, not only consultants.

# V18 The Concept of Clotheslines, 6:48
- 01:00 a clothesline is an unstructured list of bullet points; young consultants get this feedback constantly, yet it is everywhere outside consulting.
- 01:45 it reads like a brainstorm dump: the reader cannot trust that these are all the relevant points, or the most important ones.
- 02:30 fix 1: cluster the bullets into two or three groups and show the groups on the slide ("I looked at this and found three main areas").
- 03:15 fix 2: separator lines between groups.
- 03:30 fix 3: a header naming what the items are ("Key measures"); naming them also disciplines your thinking, since not every point qualifies.
- 04:15 fix 4: still text-heavy, so split by information type into columns, e.g. Measure | Description | Example. Concrete examples make it digestible.

# V19 How to Build Slides Like a Top Consultant, 9:34
- 00:15 the common failure: someone is asked for a deck, spends a week alone, returns a polished document, and the lead realises it is not what was needed. A week lost on both sides.
- 01:00 start with a sketch: draw a grid of page boxes on paper, write each page's action title, roughly sketch its layout (a chart here, commentary there, a table) and a few key bullets.
- 02:30 take the sketch to the team lead and align on it together (or on a digital whiteboard); the lead often grabs a pen too. Only then build, and the chance of success is far higher.
- 03:30 slide steps: 1 messaging (what do I want to say; the error is copy-pasting old slides together without thinking about the flow); 04:30 example title "For company XYZ we are able to pay 100 million EUR in form of an asset deal"; 05:00 2 structure (the building blocks become the layout: asset categories current/fixed/intangible, list of assets, details, price per asset summing to a total); 06:45 3 graphics or main visuals (here a waterfall per asset adding to the total); 08:00 4 supporting text (fill the bullets); 08:30 5 polishing (alignment, footnotes, quality checks), which professional services take very seriously.

# V20 3 Easy Tips to Perfectly Align Slides in PowerPoint, 10:09
- 00:30 alignment drives the first impression ("look and feel"); misalignment reads as amateur.
- 01:00 definition: elements sit on a logical grid, start on the same lines, and are evenly distributed.
- 01:45 dragging against PowerPoint's smart guides is a beginner's method and slow.
- 02:15 tip 1: add align and distribute commands to the Quick Access Toolbar (More Commands, choose "All commands"), then use Alt+1, Alt+2 and so on as shortcuts.
- 03:45 tip 2: distribute only works on whole units; group each row first (Ctrl+G, ungroup Ctrl+Shift+G), then align and distribute the groups. Left-align divider lines too.
- 06:30 tip 3: PowerPoint's default text box has internal margins that alignment ignores, so text never lines up with other elements. Format Shape → Text Box → set all margins to zero, then right-click "Set as Default Text Box". Ideally the corporate template already does this.

# V21 Audience Expectations for Presentations, 14:46
- 03:00 the "communication triangle", three things to adapt to:
  - 03:30 personal style and company culture (a big tech firm differs from an old-school industrial company);
  - 04:30 the objective occasion (boardroom versus colleagues versus an intern), each with expected rules;
  - 05:15 audience expectations, which differ even within one company: some boards accept a table pasted from Excel, others expect every page designed.
- 06:30 five document types:
  1. Team alignment: as brief and simple as possible; no elaborate storyline or designed pages.
  2. 07:30 Jour fixe (recurring cross-team meeting): still hands-on, but quality matters more because these get forwarded to people who were not there, and confusion can become political. 09:45 agree a standing agenda at the start so the document has a fixed skeleton.
  3. 10:15 Board level or senior leadership: "nail it". A board member runs 20 to 50 projects; small inaccuracies derail the meeting. 11:00 a partner's advice: as simple as possible, very basic language, no fancy technical terms, larger font sizes, no tiny text nobody reads.
  4. 11:45 External (investor relations, banks, lenders): board-level standards plus legal implications, e.g. claims about liquidity. Involve legal and PR.
  5. 12:45 Documentation: may never be presented; detailed, specific, lots of text; must stand alone completely for a reader years later.

# V22 Prepare your Business Presentations (room and logistics), 12:20
Mostly meeting logistics, not slide content. Kept for a short "delivery" section.
- 00:00 presentations start late mainly because nobody prepared.
- 01:00 mindset: the junior person makes logistics their own problem, so the senior presenter does not have to; in the room, they fix a failing device so the partner can talk with clients.
- 02:30 the room setup must fit the audience (one small microphone for a big room excludes people); when visiting, find a second microphone or borrow kit, perhaps through the sponsor's assistant.
- 03:30 dial into the video or phone conference before the start time; know who joins remotely.
- 04:15 confirm flipcharts and whiteboards exist at client sites; bring your own if needed.
- 05:00 carry display adapters (HDMI, VGA and others), a presenter clicker (05:15, so the speaker controls slide timing rather than someone at the laptop), and two portable speakerphones that pair to cover a large room.
- 07:00 online: stable internet, quiet place; never take an important client call from a train (a promotion was questioned over exactly that). 08:30 for senior audiences, spend a few hundred euros on a microphone, light and camera.
- 09:30 be in the room at least 30 minutes early for important meetings; 10 minutes is not enough to recover from one failure.

# V23 Every slide speaks for itself (key principle), 11:21
- 01:00 rule: pull any single page out of a deck and it must explain itself to anyone, because decks are shared, filed and read months or years later, often after the author has left; they are rarely presented as keynotes.
- 03:15 especially charts. Every chart includes:
  - 04:00 a clear description of what is shown (a chart title), as concrete as possible;
  - 04:15 the unit of measurement for every dimension (on a bubble chart: x, y and bubble size);
  - 05:00 "percent of what" (operating margin as % of revenue; CAGR of revenue or of profit?);
  - 06:00 the time: the year of the data or the period of a growth rate, since data comes from a source dated in the past;
  - 07:00 a legend for any colour or size coding, unless the title makes the highlighting self-evident (the two highlighted regions are the recommendation);
  - 07:45 the source, including internal ones ("Management Accounting"), because departments disagree on numbers;
  - 08:30 any other qualification needed, e.g. where a customer survey was run (global or one country), which changes what it means.
- 10:00 he catches his own example missing a year on the operating margin: check every dimension.
