# Story Loop Writer

Given raw material (events, facts, a draft, or a topic) and an audience, produce a story or script structured as one or more addiction loops, with each move labelled and the head fakes checked for hindsight logic; or audit an existing piece and mark where the loop breaks.

Learned from: The Neuroscience of Addictive Storytelling (And How to Master It) (https://youtu.be/KyC8r-zitVE). The timestamped beat sheet is
in `references/source-notes.md`; anything copied from the screen is in
`references/verbatim-assets.md`.

## Related skill

The loop governs the body. For the first three lines of a video or post, use
`hook-writer` (context lean, scroll stop, contrarian snapback); its snapback
usually doubles as the loop's first big question. When the user asks for a
whole script, run hook-writer for the opening and this skill for the rest.

## Prerequisites

- The raw material of the story (what actually happened, or the facts to convey), the target audience, and the medium and length (short video, YouTube script, email, post, talk). Ask for the audience if missing; stakes only work when they are personally relevant to that reader.

## Procedure

1. **Establish the stakes before the story starts: a character the audience can root for, something at risk, and urgency.** Stakes switch on the audience's prediction machine. Without a reason to care, nothing fires: no predictions, no dopamine, no attention. The character can be you, a client, or 'a creator I talked to'; the risk is what failure looks like; urgency is the multiplier ('within a week' beats 'eventually', 'the call was in 2 hours' beats 'later that day'). Stakes need not be dramatic, only personally relevant to the ideal viewer. Before the story begins, the audience must know what is at risk, who it is happening to, and why it matters right now. (see source-notes: t=02:37, t=08:30, t=14:30, t=15:40, t=16:00)
2. **Load a big question early and give exactly enough specific context to predict against.** The big question is the sustained dopamine drip: the audience holds an open prediction and cannot disengage until it resolves. Vague teasers ('something crazy happened', 'you won't believe this') give nothing to predict, so the later reveal has nothing to contrast against. Give enough detail that a guess forms, not so much that it is answered. The earlier the biggest question is loaded, the longer the drip runs. (see source-notes: t=03:05, t=09:32, t=16:16, t=16:50, t=17:30)
3. **Before every important reveal, back up and add context so the audience commits to a prediction first.** A reveal only spikes attention if it breaks a prediction. Stopping to add setup right before the answer creates the question the answer will then head-fake against. (see source-notes: t=17:15)
4. **Build each head fake in two steps: write down what the audience will naturally predict from the context, then deliver a reveal that contradicts it yet is obviously logical in hindsight, with clues planted earlier.** Prediction error is the largest attention spike available, and its size scales with the surprise. But a surprise with no setup reads as random and frustrates instead of clicking. The magician shows the empty hat and misdirects before the rabbit appears where it logically could have been. In the fairy tale, the dragon was pulling away and scared before it was revealed as a prisoner, so the twist landed as an 'aha', not a cheat. (see source-notes: t=04:00, t=10:20, t=11:05, t=17:51, t=18:40)
5. **At every point where a loop closes, open the next one in the same sentence with a re-hook, then repeat the loop for the length of the piece.** Attention drops at resolutions, not in the middle of tension. The casino shrinks time per hand; the writer shrinks the gap between hits. Find every place a question is answered or a section ends (the danger zones) and attach a new question, complication, or implication there with a bridge phrase: 'which would have been great, except', 'but here's the problem with that', 'and that's when I realized', 'which is exactly why'. The baton passes while both runners are moving. (see source-notes: t=05:05, t=05:37, t=11:41, t=19:00, t=19:30, t=19:55, t=20:15)
6. **Label each move in the delivered draft (stakes, big question, head fake, re-hook) and, for each head fake, state the predicted outcome, the delivered outcome, and the planted clue.** The labels let the user see the loop and move pieces; the head-fake check is where cheap surprises get caught before publishing. (see source-notes: t=08:30, t=09:32, t=10:55, t=12:00)

## Decision rules

- When the user shares an existing story, script, or post and asks why it is boring or where people drop off: audit it: mark where stakes are missing, where teasers are vague, where a reveal has no prediction to break, and every loop close with dead air after it; then rewrite the weak sections (see source-notes: t=07:46, t=14:35, t=16:20, t=19:45)
- When the material is business or educational (a client story, a launch, a metric): the loop applies unchanged; make the stakes personally relevant to the ideal reader rather than dramatic ('48 hours to fix the paid budget or blow through it') (see source-notes: t=12:45, t=15:35)
- When a twist the user wants has no clue earlier in the piece: plant one, or replace the twist; never ship a head fake that is only surprising (see source-notes: t=11:05, t=18:00)
- When the piece is short (under a minute or a few hundred words): run one master loop: stakes and big question in the first lines, one head fake, one re-hook into the close or call to action (see source-notes: t=17:35, t=06:05)
- When the piece is long (a talk, a YouTube script, a long post): load one massive big question near the start and run smaller local loops per section, re-hooking at every section boundary (see source-notes: t=17:35, t=20:15)
- When the user gives only facts or a timeline with no audience: ask who the reader is, or state an assumption; stakes cannot be chosen without knowing what that reader has at risk (see source-notes: t=15:35, t=16:00)

## Known gotchas

- Skipping stakes entirely and starting on the action. Fix: Spend the first lines on character, risk, and clock before anything happens. (see source-notes: t=14:35)
- Vague teasers instead of big questions. Fix: Replace 'something crazy happened' with specific setup the reader can predict against: who, what kind of thing, what it would change. (see source-notes: t=16:20, t=16:50)
- Random twists that shock but do not click. Fix: Check that the reveal is logical in hindsight and that at least one clue precedes it. (see source-notes: t=11:05, t=18:00)
- Dead air after a resolution ('and that was a great call. Anyways...'). Fix: Close and open in one breath with a bridge phrase. (see source-notes: t=19:45, t=19:55)
- Treating the dopamine claims as settled science in the copy itself. Fix: Use the loop as a working model for attention; do not make medical claims to the audience. (see source-notes: t=04:25)

## Output format

For a new or rewritten story, deliver the draft with each move labelled in
square brackets inline, then a short table:

```
[STAKES] ...   [BIG QUESTION] ...   [HEAD FAKE] ...   [RE-HOOK] ...

| Head fake | Audience predicts | Delivered instead | Clue planted at |
|---|---|---|---|
```

Follow with two or three sentences on what you changed and why, and one
line stating the assumed audience and what they have at risk. When the user
has not supplied a detail the loop needs (a number, a date, what was said),
write it as a bracketed placeholder shaped like the real thing and say so.

For an audit, open with this table before any rewrite:

```
| Their line or section | Loop move present | Problem |
```

Problems come from a fixed list: no stakes, vague teaser, reveal without a
prediction, cheap surprise (no clue), dead air after a resolution, loop
never reopened. Rank them worst first, then rewrite only the weak sections
and label the moves as above.

## Pacing by length (not shown in the video)

The video says "over and over" without numbers. Use these as defaults and
say so:

| Length | Loops | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Under 60 seconds or under 200 words | one master loop | stakes and big question in the first two lines, one head fake, one re-hook into the close |
| 2 to 8 minutes or up to 1500 words | one master question plus a local loop per section | re-hook at every section boundary |
| Longer talks and scripts | master question loaded in the first minute, local loops every 2 to 3 minutes | never let a section end on a full resolution |

Ending: the last re-hook should open a loop the audience can close
themselves (a question to act on, a call to action, or the lesson
restated as a new tension), never a cliffhanger the piece will not pay off.

## Audit checklist (not shown in the video)

Derived from the tactics section. Run it on any existing piece before
rewriting:

1. Stakes: within the first lines, is there a person, a risk, and a clock? (t=14:35)
2. Big question: is there a specific setup the reader can predict against, or a vague teaser? (t=16:20)
3. Reveals: does each important reveal have a prediction to break, set up right before it? (t=17:15)
4. Head fakes: is each twist logical in hindsight, with a clue earlier? (t=11:05)
5. Resolutions: after every answered question or section end, is a new question opened in the same breath? (t=19:45)
6. Dead air: where does a paragraph or sentence end with everything resolved? Those are the drop-off points. (t=20:15)

## True stories without a twist (not shown in the video)

The video's examples all have a natural reversal. Real material often does
not. Three honest ways to build a head fake without inventing facts:

- Head-fake the cause, not the outcome: the audience predicts why something
  happened; the true reason is different and you show it.
- Head-fake the scale or timing: the outcome is expected but its size or
  speed is not, and the setup makes the audience guess small.
- Head-fake the lesson: the audience predicts the obvious moral; the true
  takeaway cuts against it.

If none of these are true of the material, say so and run stakes, big
question, and re-hooks without a head fake rather than fabricating one.

## On the neuroscience (not shown in the video)

The dopamine claims are the presenter's working model and are stated without
sources. Use the loop because it describes how attention behaves in practice;
do not repeat "largest dopamine spike" style claims to the user's audience as
fact.
